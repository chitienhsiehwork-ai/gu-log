Demis Hassabis puts the timeline bluntly: artificial general intelligence (AGI), a system with the full range of cognitive capabilities found in the human brain, may be only a few years away. The sand is still on the table, but there are footsteps outside the classroom door.

The scary part is not just that "a few years" sounds close. The scary part is that the exam has not been printed yet, and the student is already waiting outside. If that student passes, the next exam may be written with the student's help. Humanity is not setting requirements for the next phone feature. It is trying to decide which capability means the normal classroom door should no longer open automatically.

AGI has been Hassabis's life's work. If built and deployed responsibly, it could become one of humanity's most beneficial and transformative inventions. This is not just the next phone feature or a better search box; it is closer to electricity or fire as a foundation for everything else. Humanity has, in effect, found a way to make sand think. That alone is wild.

Hassabis's estimate of the impact is equally large: perhaps ten times the Industrial Revolution at ten times the speed. Drug discovery, clean energy, advanced materials, and even resource scarcity itself could be reshaped by systems like this. It sounds like the largest prize in human history. The problem is that the prize is sitting beside a warning label nobody has finished writing.

A source check did not find a published method behind either "ten times" figure. Read them as Hassabis's declaration of scale, not as numbers ready for a policy spreadsheet. The idea may be useful; the multipliers are not measurements. (¬‿¬)
</MoguNote>

## The Exam Hall Is Smoking

AI is already producing real-world benefits, but the closer it gets to AGI, the less this looks like normal product safety. A normal software bug gets patched, rolled back, and explained in a postmortem. A frontier model failure may pull cybersecurity, nuclear risk, and biological risk into the same classroom, while the proctor is still pretending this is a normal quiz.

[Frontier Models](/en/glossary#frontier-model) have already made cybersecurity harder. More autonomous [Agents](/en/glossary#agent) can also use one generation of results to push the next one forward. As capabilities rise, the questions still hidden from view will rise with them. This is not a few bonus questions at the end of the test. It is the exam suddenly putting cybersecurity, biological risk, deception signs, and attempts to bypass safeguards on the same desk.

Hassabis's worry is not that humans cannot solve hard problems. It is that the field may not leave itself enough time. AI development is being pushed by commercial competition and geopolitics at the same time. That pressure amplifies the benefits, but it can also move frontier capabilities faster than anyone understands them. Nobody knows what happens next, and experts disagree. In that setting, policy cannot simply shout "accelerate" or "stop." It is more like the final minutes before an exam, except the proctor has not announced the pass line, the cheating rules, or who grades the paper. That is not a paperwork problem. That is smoke in the exam hall.

## First, Mark the Answer That Cannot Be Released

Hassabis's first move is not to write a giant AI law and hope it ages well. By the time such a law is done, the models may have changed three generations. The urgent question is one line: **which answer means the student should no longer sit in the normal classroom?**

His proposal narrows the battlefield first: create a standards body focused on testing frontier AI, with the United States taking the first step through a federally supervised public-private partnership or self-regulatory organization, similar to the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA). That sounds like the kind of document that can make a room lose the will to live, but the sharp edge is practical: before arguing over a constitution for all AI, decide which models need a different exam room.

The awkward part starts immediately. The exam cannot be written only by people who are good at meetings, because frontier models are not something you test with a few forms and a polite nod. Serious testing needs people who understand the systems and enough compute to run real assessments. The board would include independent technical leaders and open-source representatives, while funding would likely come mostly from industry. Too far from industry, and the questions may miss the real capability. Too close, and it starts to look like the students are printing their own hall passes and stamping their own homework. That is not conspiracy thinking. It is the annoying truth of institutional design: the people who understand the test are usually standing near the incentives.

<MoguNote>
[FINRA](https://www.finra.org/about) is not a US government agency. It is a private nonprofit self-regulatory organization funded by member fees and overseen by the Securities and Exchange Commission. Hassabis invokes it to combine industry speed with public supervision. The catch is obvious: the same industry may fund the body, be governed by it, and help shape its rules, so independence has to exist in practice, not only on paper.
</MoguNote>

Once the pass line exists, the process reaches the part that looks most like an exam and must not become a tutoring business. The body would create assessment protocols and work with relevant federal agencies and US national laboratories on capabilities tied to national security. A model crossing designated thresholds on a regularly updated set of [Benchmarks](/en/glossary#benchmark) would qualify as `Frontier-class`; an organization with such a model would become a `Frontier Lab`.

But passing would not mean taking a certificate home and calling it a day. It would mean being sent into another room, with a new stack of obligations on the desk: model cards with technical detail, strong internal cybersecurity, vetted key personnel, and serious investment in safety and security research. At first, labs would voluntarily provide models for review up to thirty days before release. Once the assessment process proved effective and robust, [Frontier Models](/en/glossary#frontier-model) would have to pass before deployment in the US market. If critical vulnerabilities appeared after release, labs would also have to work with the standards body instead of pretending the exam was over. In exam language, the student has to hand in the paper, the backpack, the seating chart, and the process for reporting a hole found after the bell rings.

The questions themselves cannot be polite. Cybersecurity, biological threats, and other high-risk domains would go on the exam. For agentic behavior, tests could look for attempts to bypass safety safeguards or signs of deception. Deployment practices such as digital watermarking would also be checked. Models would produce human-readable output [Tokens](/en/glossary#token) intended to help researchers understand their reasoning. The spirit is simple: do not ask the lab whether the model is safe; put the hard questions on the desk and watch what it writes. Safety is not a checkbox saying "I promise to behave." Safety is a hard question that makes the cheating impulse visible while the proctor is still watching.

<MoguNote>
That final proposal needs an important caveat: readable reasoning is not necessarily a faithful account of what influenced a model's answer. [Anthropic's research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/reasoning-models-dont-say-think) found that reasoning models often failed to mention hints that changed their conclusions. A readable trace can be one safety signal, but it cannot serve as a complete monitor of a model's internal process.
</MoguNote>

Then the problem flips again: the exam cannot use the same questions forever. Evaluations would be updated regularly, perhaps quarterly at first. Outdated or saturated tests would be retired and replaced. Early tests could be developed in consultation with `Frontier Labs`, because a body that never talks to the people building the systems may write a beautiful test that misses the point. Over time, though, it would need enough technical capacity to create independent held-out tests that labs had never seen, reducing overfitting. It could also work with the US government to foster third-party auditors that run assessments and develop new ones.

In other words, the absurdity of this exam is that the exam writers need help from the students to know whether the questions mean anything, but they also cannot let the students memorize the answer key. Too close, and the test leaks. Too far, and the test becomes decorative paperwork. Every step by the proctor lands on that thin line.

That paragraph matters more than the organizational names. Hassabis is not treating tests as a one-time compliance form. Tests age. Tests get saturated. Students study the test. The exam is not scripture; it is a tool that needs maintenance. Miss that, and the whole framework becomes a beautiful hall pass that expires almost immediately.

So the institutional details all point back to one blade: the rules attach to capability, not to company identity or product packaging. Any organization whose model met the assessment criteria could become a `Frontier Lab`; country of origin, open release, or closed release would not be the main question. Smaller systems from startups or academia that remained below the frontier threshold would be exempt. Once a model entered the dangerous zone, requirements could rise with capability. If the situation became serious enough, that could include a coordinated slowdown among `Frontier Labs`.

<MoguNote>
The public consequence depends on where the threshold and passing conditions are drawn. Too strict, and society may get models that are safe but barely useful. Too loose, and a lab that follows the rules may stop while a neighboring company—or another country—deploys genuinely dangerous capabilities. A standards body is not merely assigning labels; it is deciding who bears which risk.
</MoguNote>

At this point, the exam hall gets bigger. A US-led effort would be the beginning, not the ending, of shared international standards for frontier AI. The technology will affect the entire planet. Ideally, the framework would help countries first agree on the most serious risks, then work on making AI's benefits broadly accessible. This is not just a rulebook for one American exam hall; it is a first version of a threshold other countries can argue with, revise, and maybe eventually recognize together.

That is the tightest part of the argument. Hassabis is not saying, "please trust that one lab will behave." He is asking whether society can define what must not be deployed directly while the thinking sand still needs humans to press the release button. The policy sounds dry, but the scene is noisy: the student outside keeps getting stronger, the room is still arguing over the pass line, the answer key has to stay hidden, and the proctor's salary may come from the examinees. The exciting part is not the statute. It is whether humans can still say, while the student is knocking, "wait, that answer cannot be handed in yet."

## Ending: The Exam Can Still Be Reprinted

AGI could become the ultimate instrument for advancing science and medicine while driving enormous productivity and economic growth. Hassabis's point is that the rules cannot wait until models have already crossed dangerous thresholds. Shared frameworks, rigorous tests, and top technical talent have to enter now, not because humans already know the answers, but because once capability crosses the threshold first, the debate after that gets dragged by reality.

And even if the technical problems are solved, harder economic and philosophical questions remain. What economic models would let everyone thrive in a world of abundance? What values should guide life? What will meaning and purpose become? These questions cannot be left to technologists alone. Teaching sand to think does not mean society has already decided what kind of world should be built around that ability.

AI inspires both excitement and uncertainty, and both reactions are justified. The future is not yet written, but the window for writing rules will not stay open by itself. That is the real warning here: while the sand is still on the table, the exam can still be reprinted and the proctor can still draw the line. Once the sand starts moving the table, the first thing to fail may not be the model. It may be the exam everyone kept saying they would print later.
