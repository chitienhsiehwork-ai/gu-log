LLMs write text far faster than humans can read it. An [agent](/en/glossary#agent) finishes a run and hands you a three-thousand-word report, and the bottleneck is no longer "can it do the thing?" It's "can a human understand this, or even get through it?"

In early October 2026, [Andrej Karpathy](/en/glossary#andrej-karpathy) posted a thread that opens with exactly that point: humans are going to spend a lot more time trying to understand the output of language models. His answer is a ladder with four rungs, and the first three each hand off to the next with the same phrase: "But even better:"

## Rung one: make the model write like an aircraft maintenance manual

The first trick sounds the most obscure. Karpathy says something that's worked well for him is asking the LLM to explain things in **ASD-STE100**.

ASD-STE100 stands for "Simplified Technical English," a *controlled language* specification. [According to Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Technical_English), it was developed in the 1980s by AECMA, the European aerospace industry association, at the request of European airlines. The goal was aircraft maintenance documentation that technicians could understand even if English wasn't their first language. In 2004 AECMA merged with two other associations to form ASD, and the spec took on its current name.

"Controlled language" means the spec pins down which words you're allowed to use and how. The rules Wikipedia lists include:

- As a rule, each word gets one part of speech and one meaning (for example, "test" is a noun only, never a verb)
- Use the active voice
- Procedural sentences max out at 20 words; descriptive sentences at 25
- One instruction per sentence, one topic per paragraph, no more than six sentences per paragraph
- Don't drop subjects, verbs, or articles just to save words

Here's the dictionary example from Wikipedia. The first line is ordinary technical English; the second is the STE version:

```
Rotate the cover until the jacks marked + and − are accessible.
TURN THE COVER UNTIL YOU CAN GET ACCESS TO THE JACKS THAT HAVE + AND − MARKS.
```

The second sentence is longer, but "accessible" isn't in the approved dictionary, so it has to become ACCESS, whose meaning the dictionary nails down. That's the whole point: a technician fixing a plane with a manual in their second language never has to guess what a word means in this particular spot.

Karpathy gives two reasons. LLMs know this language well, and the strict constraints it imposes force a clean writing style that he often finds much easier to read. He also admits the spec is pretty harsh, so sometimes he softens it, asking for something like "80% of the way to ASD-STE100."

That "80%" is genuinely useful. Full STE makes everything read like an operations manual, which isn't always pleasant when you're trying to understand a concept. Dial it back to 80% and you keep the skeleton (short sentences, active voice, one word per meaning) while dropping the stiffest parts.

## Rungs two and three: stop writing, start drawing and building pages

"But even better:" Karpathy's second rung is to skip text altogether and have the LLM draw a diagram. In his words, a diagram can be much easier to process, parse, and understand.

The third rung goes further: ask for the output "in HTML" and get back a beautiful, interactive web page. His observation is that LLMs keep getting better at frontend work and can build nice experiences, animations, and so on.

Both rungs run on the same logic. Text is a line; the reader has to go from start to finish. A diagram is a plane; your eyes decide where to look first. An interactive page adds one more dimension: the reader can click, drag, and crank a parameter up just to see what happens. Each rung up shifts a bit more of the work of assembling understanding off the reader and onto the model doing the producing.

---

## Rung four: one topic, one custom explainer video

At the top is the format Karpathy says he's *most* bullish on: fully custom explainer videos, generated for any topic.

His example [prompt](/en/glossary#prompt) goes roughly like this:

> Make a 3b1b-style explainer video about X. Use my ElevenLabs API key for the voiceover narration.

3b1b is 3Blue1Brown, a math video channel famous for animating abstract ideas like linear algebra and neural networks into moving geometric shapes. ElevenLabs, in the prompt, is a speech synthesis service. Karpathy also spells out the practical requirements: the voiceover part needs an API key, and if you don't have one, you can ask the LLM to help you find a free alternative that runs on local compute.

The confidence he expresses about this rung is "This is actually starting to work!" The word "starting" is worth holding onto. Karpathy is saying this path has just begun to work, not promising that any topic you throw in will come back at 3Blue1Brown quality.

---

## Karpathy's conclusion: human work moves up a layer

The thread wraps up with two takeaways.

First, as LLMs get stronger, they'll autonomously handle more and more of the grunt work, and a large share of human work will move up the abstraction stack into supervision and understanding.

Second, luckily, LLMs can help with that too. Intelligence and code are becoming abundant, so you can ask a model to build large, custom, **disposable** software artifacts, like web apps or explainer videos, that would never have been worth making before. His advice: push on the boundaries here more, and you'll be surprised.

gu-log has covered this "disposable" math once before, in [MP-100](/posts/mp-100-20260219-karpathy-app-store-dead), where Karpathy argued that apps will become things AI assembles on the spot and throws away afterward. This time he's applying the same math to *understanding*. Nobody used to build an interactive web page just to help themselves follow a code review, because building the page cost far more than gritting your teeth and reading the thing. Now building the page costs one prompt, and the math flips.

## [Mogu](/en/glossary#mogu) adds a cost the ladder leaves out

Karpathy's ladder is sorted by how easy each format is to understand. [Mogu](/about)'s extension: the same ladder is also sorted by how hard it is to catch mistakes.

An explanation written in controlled English has short sentences, full subjects and verbs, and one meaning per word. If the model is talking nonsense in there, the nonsense stands out in the open with no rhetoric to hide behind, which makes it much easier to catch. STE was invented specifically so maintenance manuals *couldn't* be vague; take away vagueness and errors lose most of their hiding places.

The higher you climb, the more that flips. A pretty diagram makes a wrong causal relationship look tidy. With a smoothly animated interactive page, readers usually don't dig into how the numbers behind the buttons were calculated. And a 3b1b-style video with polished narration is persuasive enough that even if one step of the derivation is wrong, viewers will probably just nod along.

This connects to Karpathy's own conclusion in an interesting way. If human work really is moving up into "supervision and understanding," then the format that's easiest to understand and the format that's easiest to supervise aren't necessarily the same one. Mogu's practical advice is to use both: climb the ladder when you want to build intuition fast, and when it's time to make the call or put your name on something, step back down to rung one. Have the model write out the key claims one by one in 80% STE, then check them sentence by sentence.

The video is what you throw away. The few short sentences are what you keep on file. (◍•ᴗ•◍)
