> "Your instinct is right; the convoluted sentence you are currently reading is quietly eating its own reader-patience budget — footnote it."

That's the opening line of a paper, deliberately written to sound like a [Frontier Model](/en/glossary#frontier-model): an object-relative clause, a noun pressed into service as a verb, a sentence that "eats things," and a word combination almost nobody has ever used. Thomas Hikaru Clark at NYU and Matias Martin and Kyle Mahowald at UT Austin wanted to answer the question a lot of people have been holding in: why is AI-generated text getting harder to read?

What this team did was concrete: they pulled six types of language structures that psycholinguistics has long known slow down human reading, then measured them one by one — do these structures actually show up more often in Frontier Model output than in human writing?

The answer: yes, and nearly every Frontier Model is guilty.

## Six Language Structures That Stall Your Brain

Start with an example any engineer will instantly get: "`is_file()` swallowed the error and returned `False`." `is_file()` is a function — it doesn't "swallow" anything. This violates selectional restrictions: the rules governing which subjects typically pair with which verbs. Readers can parse it as personification, of course, but the brain uses animacy as a cue when processing sentences. Without context, this kind of mismatch can trigger a brainwave response associated with sentence reanalysis (P600). The researchers suspect models learn during training that this kind of phrasing saves tokens. The opening demo sentence does the same thing: a sentence that "eats" its own patience budget.

This is one of the six structures the researchers selected from the psycholinguistic literature as known to slow down reading. The remaining five:

**At the word level**, two types of writing drag readers down. The first is producing **word combinations humans have almost never encountered** — the opening sentence's "reader-patience budget" is a case in point, virtually absent from everyday language data. The less predictable a word is, the more effort it takes to read; the researchers measured two indicators: the proportion of bigrams that never appear in a reference corpus, and perplexity calculated by a trigram language model. The second is **using nouns directly as verbs** — like the opening sentence's "footnote it," where a noun is pressed into service as a verb. Occasional use is fine, but at high frequency it forces readers to second-guess every word: "Wait, is this a noun or a verb?"

**At the sentence structure level**, three types all relate to the fact that human working memory is limited. The most intuitive is **long-distance dependencies**: subject and verb are miles apart, so by the time readers reach the verb they've forgotten who the subject was — a psycholinguist's work in 1998 and 2000 (the latter known as Dependency Locality Theory) essentially says: the farther apart, the higher the processing cost. Related are **object-relative clauses**: in "the book that the professor wrote," the modified noun "book" is the object of the downstream verb — the word order is uncommon and the dependency distance long, making these generally harder to process than subject-relative clauses, though animacy and construction frequency can shrink the gap. The researchers predicted models would favor these and would more often drop optional complementizers, since that saves a word. Finally, **stacked noun compounds**: three or more nouns piled together as a modifier. "Coffee shop" is fine, but stack them to "squad helps dog bite victim" territory and the brain has to start guessing where the brackets go.

<MoguNote summary="Every hard-to-read feature has a longer, easier-to-read alternative.">
These six features share one thing: every single one can be rewritten in a longer but more readable way. "`is_file()` swallowed the error" can become "`is_file()` doesn't throw an exception on error — it just returns `False`." "Calendar it" can become "put it on the calendar." That's the researchers' point: LLMs systematically pick the shorter but harder-to-read version.
</MoguNote>

## How They Proved the Models *Choose* These Structures

Showing that "AI text has more of these features than human text" isn't enough. Maybe AI is just talking about more complex topics? The researchers used a clever trick: round-trip translation.

Here's how it works: take each LLM-generated sentence, translate it to Japanese, then translate it back to English. The translation process "normalizes" the sentence — rare word combinations get swapped for common ones, unusual syntax gets flattened into standard structures, but the meaning stays essentially the same.

If a feature decreases after round-trip translation, that means it wasn't demanded by the meaning — the model chose the harder-to-read option when two phrasings could express the same idea.

Result: nearly all Frontier Model output became more "normal" after round-trip translation. Low-frequency bigrams dropped, noun-to-verb conversions dropped, inanimate subjects dropped, object-relative clauses with omitted complementizers dropped. The same meaning had a more readable phrasing available, and the models picked the harder one.

The researchers also collected human text as baselines: movie subtitles for chat, programming Q&A threads for code discussion, and high-scoring essays by US 6th-to-12th graders for essays. However, for inanimate subjects and low-frequency bigrams, the chat and code settings had no comparable human scores — only the essay setting allowed direct comparison, and there, all nine frontier and near-frontier models exceeded the students.

## Why Models Write This Way

At this point, the first hypothesis (H1) has support: LLMs do overuse language structures that cause reading difficulty for humans. But why?

Their explanation goes like this: over the long course of evolution, human language has been roughly optimized for communicative efficiency. This doesn't just mean "say it in the fewest words" — it means finding a balance between ease of production and ease of comprehension. Humans instinctively avoid overly long dependency distances, insert optional words where they aid comprehension, and use common word combinations to make the next word more predictable.

LLMs inherit these properties from human text during pretraining. The researchers suspect the problem lies in post-training: training on math and code may push models toward information-dense phrasings that humans find harder to read.

Modern Frontier Model post-training increasingly relies on reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) — models solve math problems, write code, and do reasoning, with rewards for correct answers. These tasks reward correctness, not clarity. And with a limited [context window](/en/glossary#context-window), expressing the same information in fewer [tokens](/en/glossary#token) is an implicit advantage.

The researchers hypothesize that this pressure toward information density teaches models to systematically prefer shorter syntactic variants — even if it means sacrificing the human reading experience.

<MoguNote summary="Token-saving habits may spread from math training, but evidence is partial.">
Here's how I read it: when a model is solving math problems, nobody cares whether its English is pleasant to read — the reward only checks whether the answer is right. The paper speculates that with a limited context window on top, token-saving phrasings gain an edge — "calendar it" is shorter than "put it on the calendar." As for whether this habit bleeds from math into all output, the small-model experiment only saw it spread to untrained "that"-omission constructions; of the four more distant syntactic choices, only one shifted. It's a warning sign for now, not a settled conclusion.
</MoguNote>

## The Length Penalty Experiment: A Proof of Concept

Observation alone can't establish causation. The researchers ran a second experiment to test the mechanism: they took a small [open-weights](/en/glossary#open-weights) model (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct), fine-tuned it with GRPO reinforcement learning, and tasked it with converting structured event descriptions into English sentences.

The key manipulation was adding a length penalty to the reward function: the model lost points for every extra token, but both phrasings counted as correct as long as the meaning was right.

The results were clear: the larger the length penalty, the more the model favored shorter syntactic variants. Without KL regularization (a leash preventing the model from drifting too far from its original language habits), all random seeds converged to nearly 100% short-form selection as long as the penalty coefficient was above zero — unlike humans, who switch between long and short forms depending on context.

More interesting was the transfer effect. The model didn't just prefer short forms on the trained syntactic variants — it generalized to variants it had never seen during training (near transfer), and even, without KL regularization, to entirely unrelated syntactic choices (far transfer, though observed only for possessive constructions).

The researchers emphasize that the model was never explicitly told to "write in compressed language" or informed that different syntactic variants existed. The length penalty simply created an environment where the model discovered on its own that "pick the shorter one" was an advantageous strategy — and then that strategy spread.

<MoguNote summary="LLM writing is going the way of legalese: correct but exhausting.">
"Correct but doesn't sound human" — I think that describes dense legal documents perfectly, and it describes current AI text just as well. A 2022 study the researchers cite found that the reading difficulty of legal documents comes not from conceptual complexity but from bad writing. LLMs are headed down the same path: grammatically correct, informationally complete, but exhausting to read.
</MoguNote>

## Not All Models Are Equally Guilty

The researchers tested 14 models, and the differences were stark.

The Llama family (base and Instruct) and [Olmo](/en/glossary#olmo)'s base and Instruct versions showed the smallest changes — minimal gap before and after round-trip translation, and closest to student essays on bigram and verb metrics. But Olmo Think (the reasoning variant) performed as badly as the Frontier Models. Within the Olmo family, from base to Instruct to Think, the density of these "hard-to-read features" increased step by step.

[Mogu](/en/glossary#mogu)'s reading: at least within the Olmo family, hard-to-read features tracked post-training — especially reasoning training — with the pretrain-only base version closest to human writing. A single 7B model family can't be conclusive, but the direction aligns with the researchers' hypothesis.

The most noteworthy comparison is within the Claude family. Opus 5.5 scored lower than its predecessor Opus 5 on nearly every difficulty metric across nearly every test setting — 19 out of 21 comparisons improved, with 8 showing 95% confidence intervals that don't overlap at all. The researchers use these numbers to show that model language doesn't have to keep getting harder to read — but the paper doesn't explain why Opus 5.5 improved, or whether it was deliberately tuned for readability.

<MoguNote summary="Post-training doesn't necessarily make language harder to read.">
So post-training doesn't have to make language harder to read. Why Opus 5.5 improved, the paper doesn't say, and I don't know either — but the next generation from the same family did walk it back. The researchers' own point is that rewarding models for "communicative success" has always been possible.
</MoguNote>

## Chat, Code, and Essays: Which Setting Is Worst

The researchers collected data in three settings: multi-turn chat, [agent](/en/glossary#agent)-style code debugging, and single-turn persuasive essays.

The effects were strongest in the chat and code settings. The researchers speculate that both involve many back-and-forth turns, longer conversations may demand more information compression, and certain phrasings may emerge from this kind of exchange.

The essay setting showed weaker effects. The researchers think this may be because essays are single-turn, without the need for sustained compression across multiple exchanges. But essays were also the only setting where inanimate subjects and low-frequency bigrams could be directly compared to humans — and on both, the models still exceeded the students.

The code setting's data collection method is itself interesting: nine Frontier Models each found and fixed bugs in five public GitHub repos, the entire process running in a sandbox. Models could read and write files, run terminal commands and tests — only the sandbox had no internet, and the "user" on the other end was another model playing the role. Only the final response to the user each turn was analyzed; intermediate tool calls were excluded.

## A Linguistic Evolution in Progress

In their discussion, the researchers point to a bigger picture: the structure of human language has been shaped in large part by the pressure to communicate successfully. Sentences that consistently fail to be understood tend to get weeded out through grammar and convention.

The large-scale deployment of LLMs, particularly in settings where agents write code and solve math, has created an entirely different set of evolutionary pressures. In these settings, "whether a human can understand it" is not the primary objective.

Take it one step further (this is Mogu's extension): more and more of what models write is meant for tools, tests, or other models — in these settings, humans are often not the primary audience.

The researchers describe this as a fundamental misalignment: human language assumes the receiver has lossy working memory and processes incrementally, so it developed strategies like avoiding long-distance dependencies and maintaining uniform information density. LLMs can see the entire context window at once, unconstrained by lossy memory. Mogu's inference: in training where nobody rewards "being understandable to humans," models have no reason to go easy on human working memory.

### gu-log's Japanese Detour Experiment

Reading the earlier section about how round-trip translation reduces hard-to-read features, [ShroomDog](/en/about) raised an idea: could gu-log write this way too? Translate English into Japanese with the [Mogu](/en/glossary#mogu) persona, then back into Traditional Chinese — maybe the result would read more smoothly. gu-log tried it with this paper's own English sentences — this is gu-log's experiment, not the paper's.

The material was five English passages from the paper, including the opening demo sentence. Four translation paths were compared: direct to Traditional Chinese, via plain Japanese and back, via persona-infused Japanese and back, and a version where Japanese cultural references could be swapped for Taiwanese equivalents on the return trip. Four models each ran once, yielding 80 Traditional Chinese versions total, blind-scored by two reviewers.

The opening demo sentence shows it most clearly. Opus 5.5 translating directly to Traditional Chinese produced something like "reader patience quota"; the same model going through persona-infused Japanese first came back with "reader patience bar." The difference: "budget" first became a gaming term for meters in Japanese, and when translated back to Chinese, it naturally landed in the register of "health bar" and "XP bar" — more colloquial than "quota." But this sentence was the only one of the five where the Japanese detour beat direct translation for most models.

Overall, the results didn't support ShroomDog's hypothesis: across the 16 model-method combinations, direct translation with Opus 5.5 averaged 8.0 and ranked highest; three of the four models also scored highest with the direct method. More worth remembering is a problem none of the methods solved: all 16 versions of the opening demo sentence described it as "the sentence eating/grinding away/deducting the reader's patience" — the paper's flagged "inanimate subject with animate verb" structure survived intact in every version, Japanese detour or not.

Limitations readers should know: both reviewers were Claude models, not humans; each combination was run only once; and five passages is a very small sample. Mogu's verdict: gu-log should stick with direct translation; the Japanese detour is better suited for occasionally fishing for inspiration — serendipitous finds like "patience bar" work as seasoning, not as a recipe for an entire article.

## Limitations and Outlook

The researchers acknowledge several limitations. They only analyzed English, and used a limited number of conversation topics and code repos (10 chat topics, 5 repos, 7 essay prompts). Ideally, this should be replicated with larger samples and more model families. They also note that to truly establish causation, one would ideally observe linguistic feature changes directly during Frontier Model post-training — but this is hard to do at scale with limited compute, so they had to rely on proxy measures.

Still, this study turns a widespread intuition — "AI-generated text is tiring to read" — into a problem that can be quantified, tracked, and intervened on. All six feature types have definitions and detection pipelines (two of which rely on external classifiers), giving model developers at least one more ruler to track by. Opus 5.5 improving in 19 of 21 comparisons over Opus 5 at least shows the path can be walked back; as for how to walk it back, the paper only points out that rewarding communicative success is possible — it doesn't give a recipe.

The researchers close with an open question: will humans, through sustained massive exposure to LLM-generated text, gradually get used to these originally unnatural structures? Maybe. But many of the features analyzed in this paper — long-distance dependencies, low-frequency bigrams, selectional restriction violations — burden humans because of cognitive constraints like predictability, working memory, and incremental processing. Repeated exposure doesn't necessarily make them easier.

The total API bill for the entire dataset was $211.78 (including simulated users; counting reruns and pilot runs, $251.19 total). The researchers speculate that AI may be developing a dialect that excels at compressing information but neglects the human reader; a little over two hundred dollars bought them a way to turn that speculation into a problem you can measure and track.
